White-hole instability and evaporation

A white hole is not just a black hole run backwards in time — it is an unstable, transient configuration. This page derives the two timescales that govern its life: the classical instability and the quantum evaporation.

1. Classical instability

The white-hole horizon is a past horizon: matter and radiation can only exit, never enter. Any realistic perturbation — a single infalling particle, a photon, or a quantum fluctuation — seeds an instability that converts the white hole into a black hole on a timescale of order the light-crossing time of the hole:

$$ t_{\rm inst} \;\sim\; \frac{GM}{c^{3}} $$

Numerically, for a mass M:

  • M = 1 M⊙: tinst ≈ 4.9 × 10-6 s.
  • M = 106 M⊙: tinst ≈ 4.9 s.
  • M = 109 M⊙ (M87*-like): tinst ≈ 1.4 h.

This is why white holes, if they ever form, are not long-lived astrophysical objects: the instability is exponential and operates on the dynamical time of the spacetime itself.

2. Quantum evaporation (time-reversed Hawking)

By time reversal of the Hawking process, a white hole emits radiation from its past horizon with the same temperature as a black hole of the same mass:

$$ T_{\rm H} = \frac{\hbar c^{3}}{8\pi G M k_{\rm B}} \approx 6.17\times10^{-8}\,\frac{M_{\odot}}{M}\ {\rm K} $$

The evaporation timescale is identical to the black-hole one:

$$ t_{\rm evap} = \frac{5120\pi G^{2} M^{3}}{\hbar c^{4}} \approx 2.1\times10^{67}\left(\frac{M}{M_{\odot}}\right)^{3}\ {\rm yr} $$

For stellar-mass white holes this dwarfs the age of the universe; for Planck-mass holes it is ~10-43 s. The classical instability (§1) always dominates for astrophysical masses, so a white hole would decay classically long before it could evaporate.

3. Mass ejection and luminosity

If a white hole of mass M ejects its mass in a burst of duration tinst, the characteristic luminosity is

$$ L \sim \frac{M c^{2}}{t_{\rm inst}} \sim \frac{c^{5}}{G} \approx 3.6\times10^{52}\ {\rm W} $$

Remarkably this is the Planck luminosity, independent of mass — a universal upper bound on the power of any gravitational process. Real bursts would be beamed and spectrally distributed, but the total cannot exceed this.

5. Assumptions and caveats

  • The classical instability timescale is an order-of-magnitude estimate; the exact coefficient depends on the perturbation spectrum and the equation of state of any matter present.
  • The Planck-luminosity bound assumes the entire mass is converted in one dynamical time; realistic ejection is likely slower and beamed.
  • White holes are not a prediction of standard astrophysics; they appear only in the maximal (Kruskal) extension and require exotic initial conditions or quantum gravity.
  • All quantities here are model-derived or theoretical; no white hole has been observed.
